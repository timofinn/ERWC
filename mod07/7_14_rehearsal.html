Today's Protocol

Print. Read. Listen. Mark.

Here's the shape of today. Follow it in order.

1
Print a single copy of your speech

Bring your laptop to the printer and plug in directly using the cable. Once you're connected, hit Ctrl + P. When the print dialog opens, select HP LaserJet Pro — don't save as PDF, you want the actual printer. One copy. Just yours. Hold it in your hand. This is what you're reading from today — not a screen.

2
Solo read-aloud — 0 to 10 min

Read your speech out loud to yourself first. Time it. Mark any line that feels wrong in your mouth — put a star in the margin. Notice where you speed up. That's usually a sign you don't trust the line. Target: 4–6 minutes at your actual pace.

3
Find a partner — partner opens Google Docs in Suggesting Mode

Your partner opens your Google Doc on their laptop. Before you start, they switch to Suggesting Mode using the keyboard shortcut:

Chromebook shortcut: Ctrl + Alt + Shift + X to toggle Suggesting Mode. In this mode, any edits they type show up as suggestions — not permanent changes — so you stay in control of your draft.

The person delivering the speech holds the printed copy and reads. The partner listens with the doc open, typing suggested edits or comments in real time — lines that stumbled, words that felt off, moments that landed.

4
Pay attention to how the language feels in your ears

That's the whole point of reading it aloud. The language you wrote for a screen sounds different when spoken. Where does it flow? Where does it catch? Where do you have to fight to get through it? Those are your revision targets.

5
Switch roles. Repeat.

Once you've delivered, listen to your partner's speech. Give feedback using the table below. Be specific — "It was good" tells them nothing useful.

Partner Feedback

Listen. Then Tell the Truth.

Good feedback is specific. "It was good" tells them nothing. "The part about the hallway made me remember something" — that's something they can use.

What to notice What to say to your partner
The hook What image or idea stuck with you in the first 30 seconds? Did it make you want to keep listening?
The story Name the specific scene or detail you remember most. If you can't name one, the story isn't landing yet.
The significance When did it stop being just their story? Point to the line where it felt like it opened up to something bigger.
The close What's the last thing you actually heard? Did it feel like it ended — or just stopped?
Delivery One moment they made eye contact or slowed down and it worked. One moment you lost them to the paper.
Model Speech — Color-Coded

Model: "The Appointment"

A sample graduation speech — annotated to show five rhetorical devices in action.

Anaphora
Antithesis
Metaphor
Hypophora
Tricolon
Hook

High school is a waiting room. Fluorescent lights. Plastic chairs. Everyone pretending they're not anxious. You sit there long enough, you start to think the waiting is the point.

Metaphor
The hook opens with a direct claim — no "like," no "as." The comparison does argumentative work: if high school is a waiting room, then what we've been doing here is waiting, not arriving. That tension sets up the whole speech.
Story

Junior year, I had a teacher who handed back an essay with one sentence on it. Not a grade. Not a comment in the margins. Just: "I don't believe you yet." Five words. I threw the paper in my bag and didn't look at it for a week. Then I rewrote the whole thing. Not because I had to. Because I wanted her to believe me.

Significance / Turn

What did that teach me? Not how to write a better essay. It taught me that the people who push back on you — who refuse to let you slide by on half an effort — those are the ones who actually see you.

Hypophora
The question "What did that teach me?" is the one the audience is already silently forming. Asking it first seizes control of the room. The answer lands somewhere unexpected — it's not about writing. It's about being seen.

We came here to be tested. We came here to fall behind and catch up. We came here — most of us — to figure out who we were going to be when nobody was telling us anymore.

Anaphora
"We came here" is the anchor. Notice the third item is longer and heavier than the first two — that's tricolon working inside the anaphora. The anchor builds a sense of shared purpose, and the final item is the one that costs something to say.

We didn't come here to be comfortable. We came here to be changed.

Antithesis
Two parallel halves, opposite meanings. The argument isn't stated — it's inside the gap between "comfortable" and "changed." What does school do to you if it's doing its job? It doesn't leave you where it found you.
Close

The waiting room metaphor is wrong, actually. Or it's only half right. Because the appointment wasn't waiting for us at the end of senior year, it was in the classroom at 7 a.m. when we showed up anyway, it was in the revision we didn't have to make but made.

Tricolon
Three items, building in weight. The first is abstract (the appointment). The second is concrete (7 a.m., showing up). The third is the most specific and emotionally loaded — the revision we didn't have to make. The heaviest thing lands last.

The door isn't in front of us. It was always open. We just had to walk through it.

Recording Checklist

Before You Hit Record

You don't need a studio. You need a quiet-ish spot and a way to see yourself or hear yourself back.

  • Find a spot with decent sound — not right next to an open window or a loud HVAC.
  • Stand up, or sit up straight. Slouching changes your voice more than you think.
  • Have your Google Doc open but try not to read every word. Glance — don't stare.
  • Record the full speech, start to finish, without stopping.
  • Watch it back once. You don't have to be happy with it. You just have to submit it.
