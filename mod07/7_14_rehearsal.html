Wednesday & Thursday June 3 and 4

Print. Read. Listen. Mark.

Here's the rest of the week.

1
Print a single copy of your speech. Here's how:

Bring your laptop to the printer and plug in directly using the cable. Once connected, hit Ctrl + P When the print dialog opens, click destination > see more > HP LaserJet Pro — not "save as PDF." One copy's fine. Blow the font up if it'll help you deliver the speech without getting lost.

2
Solo read-aloud — 0 to 10 min

Read your speech out loud to yourself first. Time it. Mark any line that feels wrong in your mouth — put a star in the margin. Notice where you speed up. We all rush a little when we're nervous. Target: 3–5 minutes at a relaxed pace.

3
Find a partner — partner opens Google Docs in Suggesting Mode

Your partner opens your Google Doc on their laptop. Before you start, they switch to Suggesting Mode using the keyboard shortcut:

Chromebook shortcut: Ctrl + Alt + Shift + X to toggle Suggesting Mode. In this mode, any edits they type show up as suggestions — not permanent changes — so you stay in control of your draft.

The person delivering the speech holds the printed copy and reads. The partner listens with the doc open, typing suggested edits or comments in real time — lines that stumbled, words that felt off, moments that landed.

4
Pay attention to how the language sounds in your ears

Are you using enough punctuation to chop up the rhythm and direct the traffic as your verbalize? Where do you need to speed up, slow down, pause, shift pitch?

5
Switch roles. Repeat.

Once you've delivered, listen to your partner's speech. Give feedback using the table below. Be specific — "It was good" tells them nothing.

Partner Feedback

Listen. Then Tell the Truth.

Good feedback is specific. "It was good" gives nothing. "You rushed through the section where you repeated this phrase [the phrase they repeat]. So....yeah. Maybe like, don't." — that's something they can use.

What to notice What to say to your partner
Delivery This one's huge.
Did they rush? "It's not race. Slow down turbo. Take your time."
Read in an uninspiring monotone? "Are you familiar with the concept of inflection?"
Make eye contact? "Hey. Buddy. My eyes are up here."
Hook How'd they do to capture your attention? Did it make you want to keep listening?
Body Name a detail, a memory, a phrase, that jumped out at you. If you can't name a single one, the speech is too generic.
Turn Did you notice any point where they zoomed out from something personally significant to something the crowd can relate to? No? They missed a big opportunity
Closer What's the last thing you actually heard? Did it end with a bang? or a wittle whimper? If it peters out and ends forgettably, say so.

Model: "Building the Plane"

Hook

Let's see. How to slice into this pie.

Do ya'll remember the whiteboards I built earlier in the year? the ole' left right columns with the sticky notes and cutesie graphics that grew too big for their britches, eventually growing to such a span as to become unzoomable and therefore unreadable? Didn't see that one coming.

Metaphor
"Too big for their britches" is an idiomatic expression doing double duty as a metaphor — I frames the canva built digital whiteboard as something with ambitions that outran their own practicality. You'll see this come up again in my speech: the old "swing an a' miss." Trying. Failing. Trying Somethign Different. Failing Differently. Keep an eye out for that. More metaphors comin' atcha.
Story

Those early digital whiteboards, the AI feedback tool we tried in september, the notebooks, the frickin' packets yo, all of it was me throwing spaghetti at the wall. What you saw in class was a man treading water, barely able to keep his head afloat. Some nights this year I'd wake up in a cold sweat, roll outta bed at 5:30, overbuild an assignment for two hours, only to underexplain it in four minutes. Many times I'd still be fiddling with it as you walked through the door, especially 4th, who bore the brunt of my lack of preparation.

If at any point in the year my instruction felt turbulent, confusing, or misleading, that's because you were on a plane that was being built even as it was flown - by the same guy.

"This is your captain speaking. I'm gonna be honest I have no idea what I'm doing up here, somebody just gave me the keys this plane and said 'Bon Voyage'. I'mma do best to land this thing, but uh, you might wanna hold your loved ones close."

Did we make it to the ground? You tell me. Sure it was rough ride, characterized by long stretches boredom and puncutated by panic and confusion. C'est la vie. The equation's simple. Take a bit of experimentation, add dash of cheekiness, and finish it off with a little intestinal fortitude.

Be bad at something. Get down in the arena and look the fool, flailing and floundering, in front of a crowd.

All year long I tried to give you a model of someone willing to do just that: let it rip, look back at where it fell apart, and resolve to correct course. If nothing else, I hope you take that with you.
The Turn
Tricolon
Two items, "experimentation" and "cheekiness" stay brief, then build to the big one: "intenstinal fortitude," A two-word term I deliberately expand upon, adding length and weight to it, signalling that it's the most important concept of the three.

You do that, and a funny thing happens: you stumble into flow, total absorption in the task at hand, no time, no clock, no phone, no watch, the whole world fades. You don't find it. It finds you.

Ignore the culture of celebrated mediocrity that surrounds us. Tune out people who use the word try-hard as an insult. Pay them no mind. Let em' stagnate (or fear to act). Keep moving.

When I took this job, I learned pretty quick I had to kiss goodbye two twins of delusion. They answer to these names: procrastination and perfectionism. Ever meet em'? Set out to work quietly enough, and you'll hear their tantalizing whispers — "Mañana, mi amigo, mañana. What's the big deal? Do it later" "You're really gonna submit that? You can do wayyyyy better. You know that work is far, far inferior to what you're capable of."

Hush.

So, you might be thinking. "Yeah. But what's the path"? Simple. You try. You fail. You try once more. Fall seven times, stand up eight. Find where it fell apart, and patch it up; not, for the accolades, not for the status, not for the financial incentive, those won't nurture you longterm. Here's what will: your drive to grow, to attempt, to strengthen, to learn, and to exceed in all of the the places you were formerlly incapable. Lean on that. You'll get a ton of mileage out of it.

Hypophora
The question and answer set up guides audience thinking. I pose a question "what's the path" and answer it myself, tucking in more of of that repetition for emphasis. Note the authoratative tone. The certainty. Asking it for them tees them up for an easy jump to your own conclusion.

Every athlete, musician, and skilled storyteller you admire took this path. There's no shortcuts. But here's the thing, the world doesn't see the practice, the thousands of attempts, the failures, the disappointments — they see only the outcome, and think, "they're so lucky to've been born that naturally gifted." Lies. Lies I tell you. You can be that good too. It takes only a certain stubbornness, an internal certainty that you can figure it out, and the resolve to dust yourself off and try again.

Forget about the product; focus on the process.

Antithesis
Two short parallel statements, standing in opposition, and therefore balance. "forget product" / "focus process".
Close

There's a saying people used to think was an old Chinese curse: "May you live in interesting times." Right now because we are living in a moment where most adults have only a vague inkling of what the job market is actually going to look like in ten years. The old scripts are being shredded. It is entirely possible that your generation will be the first to enjoy a three- or four-day workweek while you're still in your twenties. If our institutions do what they're actually built to do — serve the people, not just manage them — we have the potential to unlock a level of freedom, leisure, and time the world has never seen.

It's not a guarantee. But a future where you have actual breathing room to find what you care about and chase it to its highest potential? That is a future worth fighting for. To build it, though, you have to be willing to get dirty.

Opportunities come in windows that are incredibly unforgiving. They fly open for a split second and shut with a ferocity that will catch you off guard. Life requires a radical sense of timing.

Don't wait until you're ready. You may never be. Don't wait until you have a plan. Think on your feet. Don't wait for the stars to align.

Anaphora
"Don't wait" repeats here. Why? Cause it's the most important idea. Don't go more than three, they get it.

Now go get out there and dance while the music's playing.

Recording Checklist

Before You Hit Record

  • Read through it it out loud.
  • Find a quiet spot — not right next to an open window or a loud HVAC.
  • Ask yourself, how can I read this thing with inflection? (rising/falling of pitch in voice)
  • Have your Google Doc open but try not to read every word.
  • Record the full speech, start to finish, without stopping.
  • Watch it back once. Did you rush? Take your time. Pause throughout.
